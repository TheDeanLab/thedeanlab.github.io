Meetings and Expectations
=========================

The lab uses recurring meetings to coordinate projects, discuss recent work, and
stay current with the literature. Members are expected to arrive prepared and to
communicate in advance when they cannot attend.

Dean Lab Meeting Format
-----------------------

The Monday Dean Lab meeting follows a two-week cycle:

- **Week 1:** a polished research presentation followed by rapid fire
- **Week 2:** journal club focused on the paper selected from the preceding
  rapid-fire pitches

Research Presentations
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Research presentations are coherent scientific arguments and opportunities to
improve the work through group discussion, not chronological progress reports.
Failures, ambiguous results, and unfinished ideas are welcome when the
reasoning is organized and communicated clearly.

.. container:: meeting-checklist

   **Quick checklist**

   - State the central question and why it matters.
   - Explain the approach and its rationale.
   - Organize results around scientific claims.
   - Separate evidence, inference, and uncertainty.
   - Conclude with confidence and remaining unknowns.
   - End with prioritized next steps and specific questions.

Motivation and question
"""""""""""""""""""""""

Explain the motivation, significance, relevant unknown, and broader context.
State the central question, hypothesis, or objective explicitly.

Approach and rationale
""""""""""""""""""""""

Explain the approach and why it is appropriate. Include material controls,
replication, assumptions, and the evidence that would support or contradict the
hypothesis.

Results and interpretation
""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Organize results around scientific claims rather than dates or workflow
chronology. For each results segment, provide a clear question, relevant data,
concise interpretation, and statement of confidence or uncertainty. Separate
direct observations from inference and speculation, and address material
alternative explanations, confounders, limitations, and negative or
contradictory evidence.

Conclusions, decisions, and feedback
""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Make conclusions answer the original question without overclaiming. Summarize
what was learned, confidence in the conclusion, and what remains unknown.
Identify one to three prioritized next steps, explain why they have the highest
value, and state the decision each will enable. End with a **Questions for the
group** slide that requests specific feedback.

Presentation standard
"""""""""""""""""""""

Rehearse the presentation and keep it within the allotted time. Use legible
figures and labels, consistent terminology and visual conventions, and enough
context for the whole lab. Make material controls and replicate information
visible. Conclusion-style slide titles are encouraged when they accurately
summarize the evidence. Keep backup slides available for likely technical
questions. Avoid unprocessed screenshots, unexplained color scales, unreadable
plots, and protocol-level detail that does not affect interpretation.

Rapid Fire
^^^^^^^^^^

Rapid fire is a scientific-triage and high-level communication exercise, not a
compressed journal club. Each presenter synthesizes a recent paper into an
elevator-pitch explanation of its claim, importance, evidence, and suitability
for a full lab discussion.

.. container:: meeting-checklist

   **Quick checklist**

   - Use one slide and finish within two minutes.
   - State the problem and central claim.
   - Explain the approach at the level needed to understand the claim.
   - Identify the strongest evidence.
   - Explain why the paper matters.
   - Offer one independent critical judgment.
   - Recommend for or against journal club.
   - Show the citation, journal, and publication date.

Choosing a paper
""""""""""""""""

Choose substantive primary research published online within the past year,
preferably within the preceding three to six months. The paper should present a
clear scientific or technical advance, be relevant to the lab or expose the lab
to a valuable unfamiliar field, be accessible enough for a scientifically
diverse audience to evaluate, and offer enough substance, uncertainty, or
transferable insight for journal club. Select a reputable venue, but give
scientific merit and discussion value priority over prestige, famous authors,
or institutional reputation. A particularly timely preprint may be considered as
a rare, explicitly identified exception, with its lack of peer review recognized
during evaluation. Read the full paper rather than relying on its abstract or
graphical abstract.

The two-minute pitch
""""""""""""""""""""

Use no more than one slide, with one central figure or schematic at most and no
animations. The strict speaking limit is two minutes, followed by no more than
one minute of clarification. Explain or omit field-specific acronyms. Answer:

1. What problem does the paper address?
2. What is the central claim or advance?
3. What did the authors actually do?
4. What is the strongest supporting result?
5. Why does the work matter?
6. Why should or should not it become journal club?

Offer one independent high-level critical judgment, such as the paper's greatest
strength, most important uncertainty, least convincing claim, or potential to
generate a valuable discussion. Do not read from the abstract, copy
graphical-abstract language, narrate figures one by one, spend most of the pitch
on methods, use unexplained jargon, or call a paper merely interesting or
novel. The pitch should demonstrate independent synthesis.

Selecting the journal-club paper
""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Evaluate the importance of the question; the strength or controversy of the
claims; the opportunity for critical discussion; relevance or transferable value
to the lab; potential to teach the group something unfamiliar; and the accuracy
and clarity of the pitch. Each person casts one vote, with reasoning available
when it would make the intellectual basis for the choice clearer.

Journal Club
^^^^^^^^^^^^

Journal club trains members to understand unfamiliar science efficiently,
reconstruct an author's logic, evaluate whether evidence supports the claims,
identify assumptions and alternatives, design stronger experiments, and extract
ideas relevant to the lab. It is not a polished narration of the paper.

.. container:: meeting-checklist

   **Quick checklist**

   - Explain why the paper and problem matter.
   - Identify two to four central claims.
   - Match each claim to its strongest evidence.
   - Evaluate the logic, controls, assumptions, and limitations.
   - Offer alternative explanations.
   - Propose the most informative missing experiment.
   - Distinguish what is established from what is suggested.
   - Translate the paper into lessons for the lab.

Context, claims, and experimental logic
"""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Explain why the paper was selected, why the problem matters, and how the work is
relevant to the lab. Summarize what was known, what remained unresolved, and the
paper's claimed conceptual or technical advance. Identify the paper's two to
four central claims before walking through individual figures. Explain the
experimental or computational logic, why the authors chose the methods, and
whether the design distinguishes competing explanations.

Claim-evidence analysis
"""""""""""""""""""""""

Match each major claim to its strongest supporting evidence. Evaluate whether
the evidence is direct or indirect, quantitative, adequately replicated, and
supported by appropriate controls and statistics when those considerations
materially affect the claim. Examine whether representative images agree with
the corresponding quantification.

Creative and critical analysis
""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Distinguish material weaknesses from minor methodological preferences. Identify
the strongest and weakest evidence, hidden assumptions, alternative
explanations, and important limitations. Propose the single experiment that
would most strengthen or falsify the central conclusion, and consider how the
study could be improved with reasonable additional resources.

Conclusions and relevance to the lab
""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""""

Distinguish what the paper convincingly establishes from what it merely
suggests. Assess whether the title, abstract, and major claims are justified.
End with the likely impact and what the Dean Lab should learn, adopt, test, or
avoid, including unresolved questions.

Audience Expectations
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

All attendees should arrive prepared and participate actively. Listen for the
central question, logic, evidence, and uncertainty; ask clarifying questions
before moving into criticism; distinguish conceptual concerns from technical
problems; offer concise, actionable suggestions; avoid dominating the
discussion; and challenge claims and interpretations without attacking the
presenter. For journal club, everyone must read the selected paper in full and
bring at least two substantive questions or observations. During rapid fire,
the audience evaluates the paper's importance, evidence, discussion potential,
and the clarity and accuracy of the pitch, with reasoning available for its
journal-club vote.

Presenters must demonstrate ownership of the scientific reasoning, not merely
show work or summarize literature. Work may be unresolved; the thinking and
presentation should not be disorganized.

One-on-One Meetings
-------------------

Bring one or two slides that make it easy to discuss:

- short-term and long-term goals
- data, analysis, interpretation, and next steps
- obstacles, delays, and frustrations that are affecting progress

If a figure is intended to mature into a manuscript panel, prepare it using the
standards on :doc:`figure-preparation` before circulation.

General Expectations
--------------------

- Be punctual so meetings can start and end on time.
- If you cannot attend, notify the PI in advance when possible.
- Come prepared for the meeting format you are scheduled to participate in.
