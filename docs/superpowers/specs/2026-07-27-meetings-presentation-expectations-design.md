# Meetings and Presentation Expectations Design

**Date:** 2026-07-27

## Objective

Update the public **Meetings and Expectations** page so that Dean Lab members
have clear, comprehensive, and scannable guidance for research presentations,
rapid-fire literature pitches, journal club, and participation as audience
members.

The page should define polished scientific communication without implying that
the underlying work must be complete. Failures, ambiguous results, and
unfinished ideas remain welcome; the expectation is that presenters organize
their reasoning and communicate it clearly.

## Scope

The update will:

- preserve the current recurring schedule, whole-team meeting guidance, and
  one-on-one guidance
- correct the Dean Lab meeting cadence
- substantially expand the research-presentation, rapid-fire, and journal-club
  expectations
- add persistent quick-reference checklist insets for all three presentation
  formats
- add expectations for audience preparation and participation
- retain the existing site theme and documentation structure

The update will not introduce a separate rubric table, change the overall site
navigation, or redesign unrelated pages.

## Dean Lab Meeting Cadence

The page will describe a two-week alternating sequence:

- **Week 1:** a polished research presentation followed by rapid-fire paper
  pitches
- **Week 2:** an in-depth journal club focused on the paper selected from the
  preceding rapid-fire pitches

This replaces wording that could imply rapid fire and journal club occur during
the same meeting.

## Research Presentations

### Purpose

A research presentation is a coherent scientific argument and an opportunity
to improve the work through group discussion. It is not a chronological report
of everything completed since the previous presentation.

### Required content

Presenters will:

- explain the motivation, significance, relevant unknown, and broader context
- state the central question, hypothesis, or objective explicitly
- explain the approach and why it is appropriate, including material controls,
  replication, assumptions, and evidence that would support or contradict the
  hypothesis
- organize results around scientific claims rather than dates or workflow
  chronology
- give each results segment a clear question, relevant data, concise
  interpretation, and statement of confidence or uncertainty
- distinguish direct observations from inference and speculation
- address material alternative explanations, confounders, limitations, and
  negative or contradictory evidence
- make conclusions answer the original question without overclaiming
- summarize what was learned, confidence in the conclusion, and what remains
  unknown
- identify one to three prioritized next steps, explain why they have the
  highest value, and state what decision each will enable
- end with specific questions for the group

### Quick checklist

The persistent inset will summarize:

- State the question and why it matters.
- Explain the approach and its rationale.
- Organize results around claims.
- Separate evidence, inference, and uncertainty.
- Conclude with confidence and remaining unknowns.
- End with prioritized next steps and specific questions.

### Presentation standard

Research presentations should be rehearsed and within the allotted time. Figures
and labels should be legible, terminology and visual conventions should be
consistent, and sufficient context should be provided for the whole lab.
Material controls and replicate information should be visible. Conclusion-style
slide titles are encouraged when they accurately summarize the evidence.
Backup slides should be available for likely technical questions.

Unprocessed screenshots, unexplained color scales, unreadable plots, and
protocol-level detail that does not affect interpretation should be avoided.

## Rapid Fire

### Purpose

Rapid fire is a scientific-triage and high-level communication exercise, not a
compressed journal club. Each presenter will synthesize a recent paper into an
elevator-pitch explanation of its claim, importance, evidence, and suitability
for a full lab discussion.

### Paper eligibility

Papers should:

- report substantive primary research
- have been published online within the past year, preferably within the
  preceding three to six months
- present a clear scientific or technical advance
- be relevant to the lab or expose the lab to a valuable unfamiliar field
- be accessible enough for a scientifically diverse audience to evaluate
- offer enough substance, uncertainty, or transferable insight for journal club

The venue should be reputable, but scientific merit and discussion value take
priority over journal prestige, famous authors, or institutional reputation. A
particularly timely preprint may be considered as an explicit exception, with
its lack of peer review recognized during evaluation.

The presenter must read the full paper rather than relying on its abstract or
graphical abstract.

### Two-minute structure

Each pitch will answer:

1. What problem does the paper address?
2. What is the central claim or advance?
3. What did the authors actually do?
4. What is the strongest supporting result?
5. Why does the work matter?
6. Why should or should not it become journal club?

The presenter will also offer one high-level critical judgment, such as the
paper's greatest strength, most important uncertainty, least convincing claim,
or potential to generate a valuable discussion.

### Quick checklist

The persistent inset will summarize:

- One slide and two minutes.
- State the problem and central claim.
- Explain the approach at the level needed to understand the claim.
- Identify the strongest evidence.
- Explain why the paper matters.
- Offer one independent critical judgment.
- Recommend for or against journal club.
- Show the citation, journal, and publication date.

### Presentation standard

Rapid-fire pitches will use no more than one slide, one central figure or
schematic, and no animations. The strict speaking limit is two minutes, followed
by no more than one minute of clarification. Field-specific acronyms must be
explained or omitted.

Presenters should not read from the abstract, copy graphical-abstract language,
give a figure-by-figure summary, spend most of the pitch on methods, or describe
a paper only as "interesting" or "novel." The pitch should demonstrate
independent synthesis.

### Paper selection

The group will evaluate:

- importance of the question
- strength or controversy of the claims
- opportunity for critical discussion
- relevance or transferable value to the lab
- potential to teach the group something unfamiliar
- accuracy and clarity of the pitch

Each person will cast one vote. A brief explanation may be requested when it
would make the intellectual basis for the choice clearer.

## Journal Club

### Purpose

Journal club trains members to understand unfamiliar science efficiently,
reconstruct an author's logic, evaluate whether evidence supports the claims,
identify assumptions and alternatives, design stronger experiments, and extract
ideas relevant to the lab. It is not a polished narration of the paper.

### Required content

Presenters will:

- explain why the paper was selected, why the problem matters, and how the work
  is relevant to the lab
- summarize what was known, what remained unresolved, and the paper's claimed
  conceptual or technical advance
- identify the paper's two to four central claims before walking through
  individual figures
- explain the experimental or computational logic, why the authors chose the
  methods, and whether the design distinguishes competing explanations
- match each major claim to its strongest supporting evidence
- evaluate whether the evidence is direct or indirect, quantitative, adequately
  replicated, and supported by appropriate controls and statistics when those
  considerations materially affect the claim
- examine whether representative images agree with the corresponding
  quantification
- distinguish material weaknesses from minor methodological preferences
- identify the strongest and weakest evidence, hidden assumptions, alternative
  explanations, and important limitations
- propose the single experiment that would most strengthen or falsify the
  central conclusion
- consider how the study could be improved with reasonable additional resources
- distinguish what the paper convincingly establishes from what it merely
  suggests
- assess whether the title, abstract, and major claims are justified
- end with the likely impact and what the Dean Lab should learn, adopt, test, or
  avoid

### Quick checklist

The persistent inset will summarize:

- Explain why the paper and problem matter.
- Identify two to four central claims.
- Match each claim to its strongest evidence.
- Evaluate the logic, controls, assumptions, and limitations.
- Offer alternative explanations.
- Propose the most informative missing experiment.
- Distinguish what is established from what is suggested.
- Translate the paper into lessons for the lab.

## Audience Expectations

All attendees will:

- arrive prepared and participate actively
- listen for the central question, logic, evidence, and uncertainty
- ask clarifying questions before moving into criticism
- distinguish conceptual concerns from technical problems
- offer concise, actionable suggestions
- avoid dominating the discussion
- challenge claims and interpretations without attacking the presenter

For journal club, everyone will read the selected paper in full and bring at
least two substantive questions or observations.

During rapid fire, the audience will evaluate the paper's importance, evidence,
discussion potential, and the clarity and accuracy of the pitch. Members should
be prepared to explain the reasoning behind their journal-club vote.

## Shared Cultural Expectation

Presenters are responsible not only for showing work or summarizing literature,
but for demonstrating ownership of the scientific reasoning. Work may be
unresolved; the thinking and presentation should not be disorganized.

## Visual Treatment

Checklist insets will:

- use a restrained blue-tinted treatment compatible with the existing Sphinx
  documentation theme
- remain visible rather than requiring an expandable interaction
- use a text label and heading so their meaning does not depend on color
- contain ordinary semantic list markup
- remain readable at desktop and narrow-screen widths
- print cleanly

The implementation should use one reusable style rather than format-specific
one-off styling.
